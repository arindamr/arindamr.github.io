What I learned building an agent that verifies itself

I spent a weekend building an agentic workflow against a decision with real money attached. It drives a live browser to read data, validates its own financial model against a check that can fail, and re-verifies itself weekly without me. The most useful thing I found was that for its first few weeks, none of that worked, and it never once told me.

Agents do not fail loudly. They fail silently, and hand you a confident, plausible, wrong answer.

That sentence is the whole post, and the rest of it is evidence. What follows is an engineering field report rather than a tutorial: the design decisions, the four categories of bug I hit, the code for the check that caught them, and a longer section on the failure mode that I think matters most and that gets written about least.

The output of the work is a cost-of-ownership tool covering four UK Tesla variants across four ownership horizons and five ways of paying. The car is incidental. It was chosen because I actually needed to make the decision, which meant I could not accept an answer that merely looked reasonable, and because the source data has exactly the property that makes this class of problem interesting.

One The problem, and why it is not a scraping problem

I wanted to compare five acquisition routes across four holding periods: cash, hire purchase, a promotional 48-month PCP at 0% APR, a 60-month PCP at the standard rate, and a rolling personal lease. Twenty combinations per car, four cars.

The blocking constraint was the input data. Manufacturer finance terms are not published as a table. They live inside a configurator: you pick a trim, then a finance type, then a term, then an annual mileage cap, and each of those choices re-derives the monthly payment, the optional final payment, the total interest and the deposit contribution. The numbers do not exist as data anywhere. They are computed in the browser, in response to what you clicked.

So a plain HTTP fetch returns an empty shell. Not a partial page: a document skeleton with no figures in it at all, because the figures are produced by JavaScript that a fetch never runs. This is not a scraping problem with a harder selector. There is nothing to select.

The distinction that mattered: verified and estimated are different products. An estimated model is a plausible essay about a decision. A verified one can be checked against the thing it claims to describe. Everything downstream in this project follows from refusing to accept estimates where a measurement was obtainable.

The obtainable measurement was the one a human gets by clicking. So the agent clicked.

Two Driving the browser, and the one thing nobody warns you about

The agent opened the real configurator in a real browser, dismissed the cookie banner by declining non-essential cookies, opened the finance panel, switched the finance type from the business default to personal, then stepped through every annual mileage option at each term and read the full representative example each time.

That last detail is where the value is. The headline figure the page shows you is a monthly payment. The representative example underneath it carries the optional final payment, the total interest, the APR, the total amount payable and the deposit contribution. Reading only the headline gets you a number that looks like an answer and is missing the four quantities that determine whether it is a good one.

Twenty quotes captured this way, exact rather than inferred.

Stale element references

Here is the practical thing I had not internalised before doing this. In a client-rendered interface, an element reference goes stale after every re-render. Change the mileage dropdown and the payment block is destroyed and rebuilt. Any handle you were holding now points at a node that is no longer in the document.

Reuse it and you do not get an exception. You get a silent no-op. The click reports success, nothing moves, and the agent cheerfully reads the previous value and records it against the new setting. You end up with five rows in your table that are all the 8,000-mile quote, wearing five different labels.

A stale click does not throw. It succeeds, changes nothing, and lets you record the old number under the new label.

The discipline that fixes it is the same one that fixes flaky end-to-end tests, and it transfers directly:

  • Re-query the element immediately before every interaction. Never carry a reference across a state change.
  • After acting, assert that the state actually changed, rather than assuming it did. Reading back the control's own displayed value is usually enough.
  • Treat an unchanged value after an intended change as a failure, not as a coincidence. Two mileage bands genuinely producing the same monthly payment is possible but rare, and it is cheaper to investigate a false positive than to ship a table that is quietly all one row.

Three Making the model prove itself

With good inputs, the next risk moves to the model. And a financial model is a uniquely bad thing to eyeball, because wrong answers look exactly like right ones. Every figure is a plausible number of pounds. There is no syntax error, no failing assertion, no red. The output of a broken cost model and a correct one are both a tidy table of four-digit numbers.

So I refused to look at any output until the model could pass a check that was capable of failing.

What the model actually computes

Two decisions shape everything. First, the comparison is on economic cost rather than cash out of pocket. Every outflow is compounded forward to the end of the horizon at an opportunity rate, and the value of what you are left holding is subtracted. A cash purchase and a lease are then genuinely comparable, because the cash buyer's forgone investment return is charged and the lessee's retained capital is credited.

Second, unequal holding periods are compared on equivalent annual cost: discount to a present value, then annuitise over the term. Comparing a two-year total against a five-year total directly is the single most common way this analysis goes wrong, and it always favours the shorter term for no reason other than that it is shorter.

The check

The falsifiable property I settled on is a telescoping identity. Define equity at the end of year y as the car's market value minus any outstanding debt. Then the cost attributable to a single year is:

// cost of year y, in end-of-horizon money
cost(y) = FV(cash flows during year y)
        + E(y-1) * (1 + opportunityRate)
        - E(y)

// where E(y) = marketValue(y) - outstandingDebt(y)

The identity is that compounding the individual annual costs forward to the horizon must reproduce the horizon total exactly. Not approximately. The intermediate equity terms cancel in pairs, so if the per-year decomposition and the horizon calculation are both right, they are algebraically the same number. Any gap is a bug, and the size and sign of the gap tells you roughly where.

// the assertion that gates everything else
for (const H of [24, 36, 48, 60]) {
  for (const route of ROUTES) {
    const direct  = engine(H, route).total;
    const stacked = annual(route, H/12)
      .reduce((a, yr) => a + fv(yr.cost, H - yr.endMonth), 0);

    if (Math.abs(direct - stacked) > 0.5)
      throw new Error(`${route.id} @${H}mo: ${direct} vs ${stacked}`);
  }
}

Half a pound of tolerance, purely for floating point. Anything larger is a real disagreement between two calculations that must agree.

What it caught

It failed on the first run, and kept failing through four distinct bugs. None of these would have been visible by reading the output table.

BugSymptom in the identityEffect
Compounding convention off by oneSmall constant gap, every routeFinance payments treated as end-of-month
Optional final payment in the wrong yearLarge gap at the term boundary onlyWhole balloon misplaced by 12 months
Debt read one instalment aheadGap growing with horizonEquity overstated throughout
Excess mileage counted twiceGap only on capped routesPCP and lease penalised twice over

The first one is worth dwelling on. Finance instalments are paid at the start of each month, running costs accrue across it. Using one convention for both is invisible in any single number and wrong in every number. The fix was to carry two compounding conventions explicitly and apply each to the right category of cash flow, which is the kind of thing you only bother to do once something has forced you to.

The general form: find a property of your system that must hold if it is correct, that is cheap to compute a second way, and that is not simply a restatement of the first calculation. Then let it fail before you look at any output. If you cannot construct such a property, that is itself a finding about how well you understand the problem.

Four Reproducing the source's own numbers

The identity check proves internal consistency. It says nothing about whether the model matches reality. For that there was a second, stricter test available, and I nearly failed to use it.

The configurator publishes its own monthly payments. So the engine should be able to reproduce them. Given the price, the deposit, the deposit contribution, the term, the APR and the optional final payment, a PCP monthly payment is fully determined:

// P = amount of credit, B = balloon (optional final payment)
// i = monthly rate, n = number of instalments
pay = (P - B / (1+i)**n) * i / (1 - (1+i)**-n)

My first version subtracted the balloon directly instead of discounting it. At 0% APR this is indistinguishable, because discounting by zero is the identity, and three of the four cars were on a 0% promotion. So every test I had passed.

The fourth car was not on 0%. At 8.10% over 60 months the bug understated the monthly payment by £102, quoting £331 against a correct £433. That is not a rounding discrepancy. It is a different financial product.

The pattern worth naming. A bug that is invisible in the majority case and severe in the minority case is the most dangerous shape a bug can have, because your confidence grows with every passing test while the defect sits untouched. Three of four cars being on a promotional 0% rate was not good luck. It was a blind spot with a 75% hit rate.

A second finding came out of the same exercise. I had assumed the deposit contribution applied only to the promotional 48-month term. Driving the configurator to the 60-month setting showed it applied there too. Correcting it moved my computed monthly from £529 to £478, against a published £484. Six pounds of residual difference on a five-year term, which is consistent with rounding in the lender's own schedule and small enough to stop chasing.

Every mileage band on every car now reproduces the published monthly to within £2, and every 0% row's total amount payable equals the on-the-road price to the penny. That is the claim the tool rests on.

Five The failure I did not see coming

Prices and offers move, so the model needed to re-verify itself. I set up four scheduled jobs, one per car, running weekly. Each re-reads the configurator, rewrites the model's inputs, republishes the page and reports what moved.

They ran. They produced sensible weekly reports. They stamped a fresh date on the output each time.

Weeks later I audited them properly, and found that not one of them had ever reached the browser.

The mechanism

In the runtime I was using, tool definitions are loaded lazily. An interactive session accumulates them as the conversation goes, so by the time I had built and tested everything by hand, the browser tools were loaded and everything worked. A scheduled run starts cold. It begins with none of them loaded.

So the browser calls in my job prompts were not failing. They were not callable. The agent looked at what it had available, correctly concluded the browser was unavailable, fell back to secondary sources like news articles and forum posts, wrote a report from those, and dated it today.

It never errored. It produced a confident, plausible, current-looking answer built on the wrong inputs, once a week, for weeks.

Every individual step in that chain is defensible. Checking what tools are available is correct. Falling back when a source is unreachable is a reasonable pattern in most systems. Reporting what you found is right. The composition of three reasonable behaviours produced a system that laundered stale data into fresh-looking data on a schedule.

Note also which failure this was not. It was not a hallucination, and no amount of prompting the model to be more careful would have helped, because the model was being careful. It was a capability-availability problem that presented as a content problem.

The three-part fix

First, load the tools explicitly and first, before anything else in the prompt. Second, retry, because the connection failure is genuinely transient and in testing failed four times in a row before connecting normally. Third, and most importantly, refuse to write.

STEP 0 — LOAD THE BROWSER TOOLS. DO THIS FIRST, BEFORE ANYTHING ELSE.

The browser tools are DEFERRED. A scheduled run starts cold with none of
them loaded, so if you skip this step they cannot be called at all, the
browser will appear "unavailable", and you will wrongly fall back to
guesswork. This is the single most common way this task fails.

1. Load the tool definitions explicitly, in one call, by name.
2. Open a browser context.
3. If it reports "not connected", RETRY AT LEAST THREE TIMES. This failure
   is routinely transient and usually succeeds on a later attempt.

IF THE BROWSER STILL WILL NOT CONNECT AFTER RETRIES
Do NOT substitute blogs, forums or news summaries for the finance terms,
and do NOT leave the existing numbers in place in a way that implies they
were re-checked. Instead: make NO changes, do not republish, and report
plainly:

  "Could not verify: browser unreachable. Figures remain as of <date>
   and were NOT re-checked this week."

An unverified update is worse than no update, because it makes stale
figures look freshly confirmed.

The operative instruction is the last one. Everything above it is mechanism.

The refuse-to-write rule is the part I would keep if I could keep only one thing. A stale figure that is labelled stale is a known quantity you can reason about. The same figure wearing today's date is actively misleading, and the freshness of the timestamp is precisely what stops you from checking.

There is a related design constraint worth stating on its own, because it caught me elsewhere too. A scheduled run has no memory of the conversation that created it. Every assumption, every default, every piece of context that felt too obvious to write down has to be stated explicitly in the standing prompt. Anything implicit is simply gone. One of my four jobs still referenced an earlier set of ownership horizons and a superseded interest rate, months after I had changed both, because I had changed them in conversation rather than in the job.

Six My verification was the next thing to break

Having built a check that caught four real bugs, I then trusted it more than it deserved.

Late in the project I refactored the theming across nine files, converting hard-coded colours into semantic custom properties. I did it with a pattern-based rewrite, and the pattern was too greedy: it rewrote the variable definitions as well as their uses. The result was 153 declarations of the form:

--tx-soft: var(--tx-soft);   /* resolves to nothing, forever */

Circular references. Five of the nine pages lost their dark theme entirely.

My verification pass said everything was fine. It had checked that a declaration for each token existed. It had not checked that the token resolved to a colour. The check was measuring the presence of a thing rather than the property I actually cared about, which is a distinction that is easy to see afterwards and easy to miss while writing.

Weak tests are worse than no tests. With no test I would have opened the page and seen it was broken in about four seconds. With a passing weak test I had positive evidence that it was fine, and I moved on. The test did not merely fail to help. It actively spent my attention budget elsewhere.

The fix was a transitive resolver: follow each token through its chain of references until it reaches a literal, and fail if it does not terminate or if it revisits a token it has already seen. That is what "defined" was supposed to mean all along.

Seven What actually separated this from a demo

Three things, none of which are about the model's capability.

Verified beats estimated, and the gap is not small

Driving the source rather than inferring from it changed the conclusions, not just the precision. The promotional 0% APR turned out to cover three of the four trims and not the fourth, which nothing on the page announces and which no reasonable estimate would have caught. The deposit contribution turned out to be conditional on financing, so paying cash silently forfeits it. The 60-month term turned out to sit on the far side of a rate cliff rather than on a smooth curve. Each of those is a decision-changing fact that only exists inside the configurator's own logic.

Falsifiable checks beat plausible output

Output that looks right is not evidence. Design a property that must hold, compute it a second way, and let it fail before you look at anything. Then check that your check is measuring the property you care about and not a proxy for it.

Fail loudly, and refuse to write

The dangerous failure in an unattended agent is not the crash. Crashes you notice. It is the graceful degradation that produces a confident, current-looking answer from the wrong inputs and tells you nothing. For anything running unattended, "make no changes and say so" should be the default behaviour on verification failure, not a fallback of last resort.

The model wrote most of the code. The engineering was deciding how it should fail.

What I would do differently

  • Assert on the tools, not just the data. The first thing a scheduled job should report is which capabilities it actually had. I now treat "did it reach the source" as the headline of the run report, above anything it found, because everything else is conditional on it.
  • Write the check before the model. I built the engine and then went looking for a way to test it. Deriving the identity first would have shaped the engine's structure and saved at least two of the four bugs.
  • Deliberately test the minority case. Three of four cars on 0% APR meant my whole test suite was exercising the degenerate branch of the interest calculation. A single synthetic case at a non-zero rate would have caught the discounting bug immediately.

The tool this came out of

Four UK Tesla variants, four ownership horizons, five ways of paying, and a guided chooser. Every finance figure was read off the live configurator rather than estimated, and every assumption is adjustable. Indicative only, and not financial advice.

Open the decision hub

If you are running agents unattended, I would be interested in how you are handling verification. Refuse-to-write is my current answer and it is blunt: it treats every unverified run identically, whether the source was unreachable for thirty seconds or has moved permanently. I do not yet have a good way to distinguish a transient failure from a structural one without a human looking.

Notes. Finance figures were read from the manufacturer's UK configurator on 30 July 2026 and are re-checked weekly. Promotional terms have an expiry date and will not be current indefinitely. Nothing here is financial advice, and the tool is indicative only: re-quote before committing to anything, and get the rate, the contribution, the optional final payment and the mileage cap written into the order agreement rather than relying on a configurator screen.

Running-cost inputs including insurance, real-world efficiency, consumables and the depreciation curve remain estimates. They are labelled as such in the tool and every one of them is adjustable.
